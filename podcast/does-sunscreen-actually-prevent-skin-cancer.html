Does sunscreen actually prevent skin cancer?

By F. Perry Wilson, MD MSCE

Short answer

Yes. In the randomized Nambour trial, 1,621 Australians assigned to daily SPF 15+ had a 38% lower rate of squamous cell carcinoma over eight years of follow-up, plus 3 invasive melanomas versus 11 in the comparison group. No chemical in sunscreen has a plausible mechanism for causing cancer. Vitamin D is the opposite story: low levels track with almost every bad outcome, but large placebo-controlled trials of supplementation show nothing.

The sun is not controversial. Your relationship with it apparently is. Somewhere between the blistering sunburns of the 1980s and today's toddlers in full-length rash guards, a counter-movement emerged online insisting sunscreen is the real carcinogen and that watermelon is a reasonable substitute. It isn't, and the randomized data on this is better than you'd expect.

How sunlight actually damages skin

Two wavelength bands matter: UVA and UVB. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy but penetrate less deeply. UVB is the short, high-energy stuff, and DNA happens to be a chromophore for it. UVB exposure crosslinks DNA bases, and while your repair machinery usually fixes this, sometimes it repairs it wrong, pairing an adenine with a cytosine instead of a guanine. On the next round of replication that C becomes a T. That C to T transition is the signature UV mutation. Land one in a tumor suppressor gene like TP53 and you're off to the races.

UVA works differently, generating reactive oxygen species, and because it penetrates deeper it does more of the photoaging work: wrinkles, sunspots, the whole business. Both are locally immunosuppressive, which is precisely why controlled UV exposure treats inflammatory skin conditions like psoriasis.

Tanning beds deliver the same thing. There's a modest case that they're worse: one molecular study found tanning bed users carried higher melanocyte mutation burdens distributed across a broader field of body sites, and they're more likely to develop multiple primary melanomas. Early age at first use adds risk. But I wouldn't call either one safe. If you want to look tan, there's a spray for that.

The sunscreen conspiracy, and the trial that answers it

The argument you see online, usually delivered shirtless, goes like this: sunscreen came into wide use after 1980, melanoma rates have climbed 700% since, therefore sunscreen causes cancer. This is an ecological fallacy with a large helping of reverse causality on top. Tanning barely existed as an aesthetic goal in the 1930s and 40s. Before cheap travel, wealthy people signaled status by being pale, proof they didn't work in a field. Then the ideal flipped, people started deliberately baking themselves, and they wore sunscreen to avoid the burns while they did it. Detection also improved enormously. In 1850, a melanoma just killed you and nobody wrote it down as melanoma.

None of the chemicals in sunscreen have been associated with cancer. None damage DNA. None have a mechanism by which they could. There was a single recall for benzene contamination, which is a manufacturing problem, not a pharmacology one. The one criticism I'll grant partial credit is the false-sense-of-security argument: sunscreen may lead you to stay out longer and reapply less than you should. That's a behavior problem, not a carcinogen.

Then there's the randomized evidence. The Nambour trial, published in the Lancet in 1999, randomized 1,621 Australians to daily SPF 15+ or discretionary use over 4.5 years. Over eight years of follow-up, the daily-use group had a 38% lower rate of squamous cell carcinoma. No effect on basal cell carcinoma. There were 3 invasive melanomas in the sunscreen group versus 11 in the comparison group, not enough to be statistically confident given how uncommon melanoma is, but meaningful, along with roughly 50% fewer non-invasive melanomas. And that was SPF 15, which is low by current standards.

Observational studies on sunscreen and melanoma are all over the map, and influencers can cherry-pick either direction. The reason is confounding by indication, or as the economists prefer, reverse causality. People at higher risk use more sunscreen. It's the same reason people on statins have more heart attacks than people who aren't on statins.

Which sunscreen, and how much

Mineral sunscreens work as a physical barrier, reflecting UV off tiny particles. Only zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are FDA-approved here. Zinc covers both UVA and UVB; titanium is mostly UVB, so if you care about photoaging you want something else in the mix. Mineral filters don't break down over time. Nanoparticle formulations leave less of the 18th-century-consumptive look and appear to work just as well.

Chemical filters, 16 of them approved, vary in what they block. Avobenzone, for example, is mostly UVA. SPF is driven largely by UVB, so the number on the bottle doesn't tell you about UVA coverage. Look for "broad spectrum," which is the FDA-regulated label. As for endocrine disruption fears: feed a rat enormous doses of these chemicals for its whole life and yes, its reproductive system suffers. Reaching that exposure through normal topical use isn't feasible.

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours. You do not need SPF 100. The USPSTF recommends behavioral counseling for fair-skinned people under 24 to reduce sun exposure. Use whichever formulation you'll actually put on, which is the only property that matters in practice.

Vitamin D: the sun makes plenty, the pills do nothing

UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in your skin to vitamin D3. The liver turns that into 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and the kidney into 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, the only biologically active form. Twenty minutes of whole-body UVB in a light-skinned person produces roughly 10,000 IU. Typical supplements contain 1,000 to 2,000 IU. You cannot overdose this way, because sunlight degrades excess D3 in the skin. Oral supplements have no such brake, and high doses can drive calcium up enough to cause kidney damage, arrhythmias, and coma.

So why does low vitamin D associate with cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, falls, COVID, depression, and all-cause mortality, while randomized supplementation trials with 25,000-plus participants show essentially nothing? Because vitamin D level is a lifestyle biomarker. The person with a high level goes outside, exercises, and eats a varied diet. The person with a low level is often sick, sedentary, or nutritionally depleted. Making the marker look better doesn't fix what the marker was pointing at.

Darker-skinned people synthesize less D3 for the same exposure, since melanin blocks the UVB doing the work. Winter and high latitudes cut it too. That's a reason to think about dietary intake. It is still not a reason to expect much from a supplement, because the trials don't show benefit in any skin tone.

Mood, myopia, and the perineum question

UK Biobank data on more than 400,000 people found each additional hour spent outdoors during the day associated with lower odds of major depressive disorder, odds ratios around 0.92 to 0.98, plus less anhedonia and low mood. Observational, so treat it gently. Seasonal affective disorder is real and responds to blue light therapy in randomized trials. Notably, vitamin D supplementation does not treat it. The mechanism runs mostly through circadian and sleep disruption, which is why the light needs to happen early in the morning.

There are strong and consistent links between low childhood sun exposure and later multiple sclerosis, roughly 30% of it apparently mediated by vitamin D with other immune pathways implicated. Most of that literature is case-control, which is hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Outdoor time also reduces myopia in kids, and some of that comes from randomized data, though it's probably about being outside rather than the sun specifically.

Which brings us to butthole sunning, a practice with an enthusiastic online following and a claimed testosterone benefit. It doesn't work. Human testosterone levels do peak in late summer and early autumn observationally, but that has multiple confounders and nothing to do with your perineum. There is weak rat data on photobiomodulation to skin generally, not genitals, and negative data in rams. Meanwhile perineal and genital skin is thin and unusually UV-sensitive: whole-body UVA/UVB therapy for psoriasis was linked to a four-fold increase in genital tumors, which is why guidelines now say to shield that area during treatment. Dermatology figured out to cover it up. Instagram is working on uncovering it.

Bottom line

Sunscreen works. One good randomized trial, 38% less squamous cell carcinoma, and no plausible mechanism for harm. Pick broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, mineral or chemical, whichever you'll reapply. Get outside, because outdoor time tracks with better mood and less myopia in kids, and 20 minutes gets you ten times the vitamin D a pill would. Skip the vitamin D supplement; the randomized trials are clear that it doesn't do what the observational data promised. And keep your pants on.

Frequently asked questions

Does sunscreen cause cancer?

No. None of the chemicals in sunscreen have been associated with cancer, none damage DNA, and none have a mechanism by which they could cause it. There was a single recall for benzene contamination, which was a manufacturing problem. The claim that rising melanoma rates since 1980 prove sunscreen is harmful ignores that deliberate tanning became popular over the same period and that detection improved enormously.

Is mineral or chemical sunscreen better?

Both work, and the best one is the one you will actually apply and reapply. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide as a physical barrier and do not break down over time, though titanium covers mostly UVB. Chemical filters vary in coverage, so look for the FDA-regulated "broad spectrum" label rather than relying on the SPF number, which is driven mainly by UVB.

What SPF do I actually need?

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours. You do not need SPF 100. For context, the Nambour randomized trial that showed a 38% reduction in squamous cell carcinoma used only SPF 15.

How much sun do you need for vitamin D?

About 20 minutes of whole-body UVB exposure in a light-skinned person produces roughly 10,000 IU of vitamin D3, roughly ten times what a typical 1,000 to 2,000 IU supplement provides. You cannot overdose this way, because sunlight degrades excess D3 in the skin. Darker skin, winter, and high latitudes all reduce synthesis substantially.

Should I take a vitamin D supplement?

The randomized evidence says no. Low vitamin D levels associate with cancer, heart disease, diabetes, falls, COVID, and all-cause mortality in observational studies, but placebo-controlled trials with more than 25,000 participants show essentially no benefit from supplementation. Vitamin D level behaves as a lifestyle biomarker, and improving the marker does not appear to improve the outcomes it tracks.

Does sunning your perineum boost testosterone?

No. Testosterone levels do peak observationally in late summer and early autumn, but that has multiple confounders and nothing to do with genital sun exposure. The relevant risk is real: whole-body UVA/UVB therapy for psoriasis was linked to a four-fold increase in genital tumors, which is why guidelines now recommend shielding that area during treatment.

Are tanning beds worse than the sun?

Both clearly raise skin cancer risk, and neither is safe. One molecular study found tanning bed users had higher melanocyte mutation burdens spread across a broader field of body sites, and they are more likely to develop multiple primary melanomas. Early age at first use adds further risk.
