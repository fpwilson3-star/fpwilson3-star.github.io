Does sunscreen actually prevent skin cancer, or is it a scam?

By F. Perry Wilson, MD MSCE

Short answer

Yes, sunscreen prevents skin cancer, and the best evidence is a randomized trial. In the Nambour trial, 1,621 Australians assigned daily SPF15+ had a 38% lower rate of squamous cell carcinoma and roughly a third as many invasive melanomas as the discretionary-use group. The "sunscreen causes cancer" claim rests on one benzene contamination recall, not the product itself. Skin cancer rates rose since the 1980s because tanning got more popular, not because sunscreen failed.

Scroll social media long enough and you'll meet a shirtless man explaining that sunscreen causes the cancer it claims to prevent. His evidence is that melanoma rates climbed after sunscreen got FDA approval around 1980. This is a real argument people make, and it is wrong in an interesting way.

How the sun actually causes skin cancer

Two kinds of ultraviolet light matter here. UVB is absorbed directly by your DNA. It cross-links DNA bases, and most of the time your cells repair the damage correctly. Sometimes they don't. A misrepair pairs an adenine with a cytosine instead of a guanine, and when the cell divides you get a C-to-T transition. That is the signature UV mutation. Land one of those in a tumor suppressor gene like TP53 and you're off to the races.

UVA does its damage differently, generating reactive oxygen species, and it penetrates deeper, which is why it drives more of the photo-aging. Both types are also locally immunosuppressive, which is exactly why controlled UV works as therapy for inflammatory skin conditions like psoriasis.

Tanning beds are at least as bad as the sun, probably worse. A molecular study found that tanning bed users carried higher melanocyte mutation burdens with pathogenic mutations spread across a broader field of body sites. They're also more likely to develop multiple primary melanomas, and starting young adds to the risk.

The "sunscreen causes cancer" claims, one at a time

Melanoma rates did rise after 1980. So did tanning. Attributing the increase to sunscreen while ignoring the deliberate pursuit of a tan is like blaming seatbelts for car crashes.

The chemical scare rests on benzene, which critics correctly note is not on any ingredient list. It isn't supposed to be. Benzene showed up as a manufacturing contaminant in one round of recalls. That is a quality-control failure, the kind that hits many product categories, not a property of sunscreen itself. There is no evidence that the filters themselves are dosing you with a carcinogen.

The one criticism with any teeth is the false sense of security argument, the idea that people who apply sunscreen then stay out too long. That's a behavior problem, not a chemistry problem.

What the randomized evidence shows

The strongest data come from the Nambour trial, published in the Lancet in 1999. Researchers randomized 1,621 Australians to daily SPF15+ or discretionary use over 4.5 years. The daily-sunscreen group had a 38% lower rate of squamous cell carcinoma over eight years of follow-up. There was no effect on basal cell carcinoma, but there were 3 invasive melanomas versus 11 in the comparison group, and roughly 50% fewer non-invasive melanomas.

Observational studies on sunscreen and melanoma are all over the map, mostly because of confounding by indication. The people who slather on sunscreen tend to be the fair-skinned people who burn easily and already carry higher risk. That muddies the correlation. The randomized trial does not have that problem, which is why it carries the weight.

Which sunscreen, and how to use it

Mineral sunscreens form a physical barrier. Only zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are FDA-approved in this category. Zinc covers a broader UVA and UVB range; titanium is mostly UVB. Nanoparticle versions leave less white residue and are probably fine, and they don't break down over time.

Chemical filters are a different animal. Sixteen are approved, and they vary in what they block. Avobenzone, for instance, is mostly UVA. SPF is driven mainly by UVB, so a high SPF number doesn't guarantee UVA coverage. If you care, look for "broad-spectrum" on the bottle, which is the approved labeling for products that cover both.

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours. The USPSTF focuses on behavioral counseling for fair-skinned people under 24 to reduce sun exposure in the first place.

One more thing, since the internet raised it. Sunning your perineum does nothing useful for testosterone. Human testosterone does peak in late summer and early autumn observationally, but the confounders are obvious. And perineal skin is unusually UV-sensitive. Therapeutic UV for psoriasis was linked to a four-fold increase in genital tumors, which is why the standard advice is to cover those areas during treatment. Point the sun elsewhere.

Bottom line

Sunscreen works. A randomized trial showed it cuts squamous cell carcinoma by 38% and reduces melanoma. The benzene story is about one contamination recall, not the product, and the melanoma increase since 1980 tracks with tanning, not with sunscreen use. Use a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, reapply it, and skip the theatrical distrust. The person telling you sunscreen is poison, usually with his shirt off, is confusing a correlation with a conspiracy.

Frequently asked questions

Does sunscreen really prevent skin cancer?

Yes. The randomized Nambour trial assigned 1,621 Australians to daily SPF15+ or discretionary use and found a 38% lower rate of squamous cell carcinoma over eight years of follow-up. The daily-sunscreen group also had 3 invasive melanomas versus 11 in the comparison group and about 50% fewer non-invasive melanomas. There was no effect on basal cell carcinoma.

Why did skin cancer rates go up after sunscreen was introduced?

Melanoma rates climbed after 1980, but so did deliberate tanning. Critics blame sunscreen for the rise while ignoring that tanning became far more popular over the same period. The increase tracks with sun-seeking behavior, not with sunscreen use.

Is there benzene in sunscreen, and is it dangerous?

Benzene is not a listed ingredient in sunscreen. It appeared as a manufacturing contaminant in one round of recalls, which is a quality-control failure that affects many product categories. There is no evidence that the sunscreen filters themselves expose you to a carcinogen.

What SPF should I use and how often should I reapply?

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours. Because SPF is driven mainly by UVB, look for the word broad-spectrum on the bottle to make sure you are also covered against UVA.

Is mineral sunscreen better than chemical sunscreen?

Both work, they just work differently. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide, the only two FDA-approved options in that category, and they do not break down over time. Chemical filters vary in coverage, so a high SPF number alone does not guarantee UVA protection, which is why broad-spectrum labeling matters.

Are tanning beds worse than the sun for skin cancer?

Tanning beds are at least as harmful as the sun and probably worse. A molecular study found tanning bed users had higher melanocyte mutation burdens and pathogenic mutations across a broader field of body sites, and they are more likely to develop multiple primary melanomas.
