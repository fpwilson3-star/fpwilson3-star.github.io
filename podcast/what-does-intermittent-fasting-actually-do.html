What does intermittent fasting actually do to your body?

By F. Perry Wilson, MD MSCE

Short answer

Mostly, it makes you eat fewer calories, and that is where nearly all of the benefit comes from. In randomized trials, alternate-day fasting beat eat-whatever-you-want by about 3.3 kg and calorie counting by about 2 kg, but when calories are matched, fasting and regular eating produce the same weight loss. It does not reset your metabolism (resting metabolic rate goes down, not up), does not sharpen focus in adults, and has no human evidence behind the longevity or cancer claims made online.

Intermittent fasting is sold as two different things at once. One is a simple eating schedule. The other is a metabolic reset button that burns fat, cleans out your cells, boosts growth hormone, sharpens your mind, and extends your life. The first one is real. The second one is mostly a story told over footage of someone drinking black coffee.

Before any of the claims make sense, you need to know what actually happens inside you when you stop eating.

The timeline of not eating

For roughly the first six hours after a meal, nothing interesting happens. You digested food, a lot of it became glucose, and whatever you didn't burn right away got stashed as glycogen in your liver. That's short-term storage. Beyond that, the surplus goes into long-term storage, which is fat.

Around 18 hours in, if you're sitting at a desk and not exercising, the liver glycogen is gone. If you're exercising, much sooner. That's the wall runners hit on a long effort.

Now your body has a problem. Everything runs on glucose, and the brain is an expensive customer, burning something like 20% to 25% of your calories. With glycogen gone, you have two remaining sources: fat, which gets broken down into keto acids, and protein, which means muscle. That process of making fuel out of your own tissue has a technical name in physiology and a plainer one in English. You are digesting your own body.

Here's the part that doesn't make it into the influencer explainers. The studies vary, but muscle breakdown appears to outpace fat breakdown in roughly the 18 to 24 hour window, before lipolysis really ramps up. That's exactly where most intermittent fasting protocols live. There's an argument to be made that a medium-duration fast is the worst of both: you lose lean mass without having gotten very far into losing fat.

Weight loss: it works, for the obvious reason

You lose weight by consuming fewer calories than you burn. Intermittent fasting produces short-term weight loss the way nearly every diet produces short-term weight loss, by making people eat less. In trials that matched calories between an intermittent-fasting schedule and ordinary eating, the weight loss matched too.

Numbers help. A 2025 network meta-analysis in the BMJ pooled randomized trials across the whole family of approaches: alternate-day fasting, 5:2, time-restricted eating. The cleanest comparison was alternate-day fasting versus eating whatever you want, and the fasting group lost about 3.3 kg, eight or nine pounds. Against calorie counting, the advantage shrank to about 2 kg, and that wasn't a matched-calorie comparison either. It was people doing their best to cut intake, which is hard.

Successful diets work by making eating harder. Atkins made a long list of foods off-limits. You could probably invent a diet where you eat only yellow foods, unlimited quantities, and people would lose weight, because it's a nuisance. Fasting's version of this is unusually simple: no list to memorize, just a clock. If that simplicity is what finally gets you to eat less, fine. It's not better than other ways of eating less.

And the same caveat applies here as everywhere in the diet literature. Most of the weight comes back. People struggle to sustain dietary change without medication, which is a large part of why the GLP-1 drugs have been so successful.

The metabolic claims don't hold up

"It resets your metabolism" is the most common claim and one of the weakest. People doing these diets do show lower insulin levels, better insulin sensitivity, lower LDL and higher HDL, with alternate-day fasting outperforming time-restricted eating. Compared to an equivalent amount of calorie restriction, there's no difference.

Basal metabolic rate is the more interesting question, because what everyone actually wants is to burn more calories sitting still. The data go the other way. The more you fast, the lower your resting metabolic rate drops. Of course it does. Your body reads food scarcity and turns the thermostat down. For most of our evolutionary history that was the correct response.

Ketones get similar treatment. They're genuinely fascinating molecules, and the fact that your brain can run on them is a real adaptation to famine. But they are not, despite what you'll read, the brain's preferred fuel. They're the emergency fuel. Ketosis does have a legitimate medical use: a very strict ketogenic diet is one treatment for epilepsy, particularly in children, and that line of research came directly out of observations that fasting kids with epilepsy improved. It is a brutally difficult diet to maintain.

Ketones also do signal. They have receptors, and they change how cells behave. Their main metabolic signal is to slow fat degradation and suppress muscle building, because a famine is not the moment to invest in new tissue. They are telling your body to conserve, not to get shredded.

Autophagy, inflammation, and the mice problem

Autophagy is the process by which cells digest their own worn-out parts, and when it runs poorly, risks of cancer and neurodegeneration go up. So you want it happening. The headline finding is a 2010 study in the journal Autophagy showing that mice deprived of food for 16 to 24 hours had more autophagy in their brains.

Mice are not people. More specifically, mice have a far higher metabolic rate, so 24 hours without food for a mouse is closer to a human fasting for something like a week. The conversion is hard to pin down, but it isn't one to one.

The human study had 11 adults with overweight, crossed over between a normal eating window and a restricted one, and found a 22% increase in expression of one autophagy gene. A gene going up 22% in 11 people tells you very little about anything happening in the brain. It remains unknown whether this beats plain calorie restriction, whether the cells being consumed are the ones you'd want consumed, or whether the effect size on an outcome anyone cares about would be anything other than vanishingly small.

Inflammation follows the same pattern. A 2019 Cell study fasted 12 healthy volunteers for 19 hours and found a sharp drop in circulating monocytes, a type of immune cell, with similar findings in mice. The mouse data suggest they return after eating. Meta-analysis of randomized trials finds tiny statistical reductions in some inflammatory markers and no change in many others.

Then ask whether down is good. The immune system is metabolically expensive. When you stop fueling your body, it stops doing things it doesn't consider urgent, and with no infection present, dialing down immune activity is a reasonable call. That's a different statement from "fasting reduces harmful inflammation." It might equally mean that when your kid coughs in your face, your immune system took the afternoon off. Inflammation is your immune system doing its job.

On the folk wisdom: feed a cold, starve a fever. The best test is a 2016 Cell study that gave mice either a bacterial or a viral infection, then randomized them to no food or to nutritional support. The starved mice survived longer with bacterial infection and died more often with viral infection. Since most fevers you encounter are viral, the saying has it backwards. There is some logic to the bacterial half, since bacteria use substrate in your blood to grow, and there's ICU data on delayed versus early tube feeding in severe bacterial infection suggesting benefit to waiting. That isn't standard of care, and there's nothing comparable for outpatients. If you have the flu and you trust mouse data, eat something.

One aside, as a kidney doctor. Dry fasting means fasting without water. Don't do it. It's bad for your kidneys, bad for your brain, and bad for the rest of you.

Focus, performance, and longevity

The claim that fasting gives you laser focus doesn't survive contact with the data. A 2025 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin covering about 3,500 adults found essentially no effect of a 12-hour fast on attention, inhibitory control, or memory. Not better, not worse. Which is itself a little surprising, since you might expect hungry people to be distracted. Compare that to sleep, where performance degrades steadily and badly the longer you go. Your brain is extraordinarily well defended against going without food.

With one exception. Kids and younger people do show cognitive decrements when fasted. They have higher resting metabolic rates per kilogram, smaller bodies, smaller livers, less glycogen. That's a real argument for school breakfast programs, since kids who don't get breakfast at home perform worse on tests.

On athletic performance, a study of resistance-trained men on a 16:8 schedule with adequate calories found a decline in fat mass and also a significant drop in testosterone. That tracks with the physiology. Testosterone is anabolic, and anabolism is not what a food-scarce body wants to do. A trial in active women didn't show much difference either way. And a meta-analysis of Ramadan studies, a clever natural experiment, found a small hit to sprint performance and no change in aerobic performance, jump height, or fatigue. Sifan Hassan did her marathon training fasted through Ramadan and won at the Olympics, though not because of it.

Longevity is the big one. Severe calorie restriction extends lifespan in basically every species tested, from yeast and nematodes to fruit flies, mice, and primates. It has never been replicated in humans, for two reasons: people aren't in cages and can't reliably cut calories, and no one is running a hundred-year trial. The best human attempt is a 2023 Nature Aging study that assigned 220 people to 25% calorie restriction for two years. They managed 12%, because it's hard. One epigenetic clock suggested they aged two years less over those two years. The other two clocks showed nothing. I'm skeptical of epigenetic clocks generally, and I don't think they mean much at the individual level. Also, that study was about calories, not fasting.

For fasting specifically, the cleanest evidence is a mouse study that randomized animals to different degrees of calorie restriction delivered either as less food spread across the day or as fasting. Fewer calories, longer life. Layering fasting on top of the same calorie reduction changed nothing. Calories matter. Fasting doesn't.

Where the claims turn dangerous

Prolonged fasting, meaning days rather than hours, is where the online advice stops being merely overstated.

Growth hormone is the gateway claim. A 1988 Journal of Clinical Investigation study put six healthy men through a five-day complete fast, water only, and growth hormone tripled. That part is true and gets repeated endlessly. Here's the part that doesn't: IGF-1, the downstream hormone that actually tells muscle to grow, fell. Growth hormone has many jobs besides signaling IGF-1, and one of them is breaking down fat for fuel. That's what it was doing. A starving body does not upregulate muscle building in the middle of a famine. It would be a terrible design if it did.

A five-day fast is starvation. It is biologically active, in the sense that a great many markers will change. Of course they change. It's a famine. That is not the same as those changes being good. And the body composition math is unkind: in these studies, about two-thirds of the mass lost is lean tissue and one-third is fat, roughly the inverse of the ratio you see with other weight-loss methods.

The record holder for going without food is Angus Barbieri, who in the 1960s went 382 days on water, tea, coffee, vitamins, and some potassium and sodium. He started at around 450 pounds and ended at 180, and kept much of it off. He also had a bowel movement roughly every 37 to 48 days. This was called therapeutic starvation and was briefly in vogue. It was abandoned because other people on the protocol died, of heart failure, arrhythmias, and lactic acidosis.

Then there's cancer. There are reels circulating in which someone says that if they had stage 4 cancer, they would stop eating for 20 to 30 days to starve the tumor, and asks whether chemotherapy, which kills your own cells too, is really the better sledgehammer. There is no human data showing prolonged fasting changes cancer outcomes. None. And setting the absent evidence aside, after cancer type and stage, the single best predictor of survival is performance status. The weaker you are during treatment, the more likely you are to die. People with advanced cancer already struggle to eat and to hold weight, and losing weight is a consistent predictor of mortality. Telling them to fast for a month is as irresponsible as health advice gets.

There has been legitimate research into short fasts for chemotherapy toxicity. Mouse data suggested that fasting for under a day before treatment reduced symptoms. It didn't replicate well in humans. The DIRECT 2 trial, published in Nature Communications in 2020, randomized 131 patients with stage 2 to 3 breast cancer to a dramatically reduced-calorie diet for four days before chemo. The primary outcome was toxicity, and there was no difference in hair loss or nausea. A post-hoc analysis suggested a higher rate of complete or partial radiologic response in the fasting group. One small study. The trial also excluded people whose BMI was too low, as it should have. The American Society of Clinical Oncology says the evidence is insufficient for or against short fasting before chemotherapy. If skipping food for 16 hours before an infusion helps your nausea, that seems fine. It is not a substitute for treatment.

Some people should not do this at all: anyone with an eating disorder or a history of disordered eating, anyone on diabetes medication because of hypoglycemia risk, older adults already losing lean mass, people with gout, since ketones compete with uric acid for excretion, and kids and teens.

Black coffee is allowed on most protocols, for whatever that's worth. It worked for Angus.

Bottom line

When you evaluate intermittent fasting, the question is always: compared to what? The honest comparison is calorie restriction, and against that benchmark, fasting adds essentially nothing. It doesn't raise your metabolic rate, it lowers it. It doesn't make your brain run better on ketones. It doesn't sharpen cognition in adults. It doesn't extend life in the one animal experiment designed to separate it from calories. The growth hormone story is half a story.

What it does is make eating less simpler for some people, and taking in fewer calories is generally good. On that basis I'll endorse it, narrowly, as one workable way to eat less. Emily lands on the other side, mostly because the overselling has gotten disturbing, and she's not wrong about the overselling.

Frequently asked questions

Is intermittent fasting better than counting calories for weight loss?

No. When trials match calories between an intermittent fasting schedule and ordinary eating, the weight loss is the same. In the 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis, alternate-day fasting beat eating whatever you want by about 3.3 kg and beat calorie counting by about 2 kg, and that second comparison was not calorie-matched. Fasting works because it makes you eat less, not because of anything special about the timing.

Does fasting speed up your metabolism?

It does the opposite. The data show that the more you fast, the lower your resting metabolic rate goes, which is the expected response to a food-scarce environment. Insulin sensitivity and cholesterol do improve on these diets, but not beyond what an equivalent amount of calorie restriction produces.

How long do you have to fast before your body burns fat?

Liver glycogen typically runs out around 18 hours if you are not exercising, and much sooner if you are. After that your body makes fuel from both fat and muscle. Studies vary, but muscle breakdown appears to outpace fat breakdown in roughly the 18 to 24 hour window, before fat breakdown really ramps up, which is exactly where most intermittent fasting protocols sit.

Does intermittent fasting trigger autophagy in humans?

The strong evidence is in mice, where 16 to 24 hours without food increased brain autophagy in a 2010 study. Mice have much higher metabolic rates, so a 24-hour mouse fast is closer to a human going a week. The human study had 11 adults and found a 22% rise in expression of one autophagy gene, which tells us very little about cancer or neurodegeneration risk.

Does fasting raise growth hormone and build muscle?

It raises growth hormone but does not build muscle. In a 1988 study, six men on a five-day water-only fast tripled their growth hormone, but IGF-1, the hormone that actually signals muscle growth, fell. The growth hormone rise was driving fat breakdown for fuel. In prolonged fasts, roughly two-thirds of the mass lost is lean tissue.

Who should not try intermittent fasting?

Anyone with an eating disorder or disordered eating patterns, anyone taking diabetes medications because of hypoglycemia risk, older adults already losing lean mass, people with gout since ketones compete with uric acid for excretion, and kids and teens. Dry fasting, meaning no water, should not be done by anyone. And there is no human evidence that prolonged fasting helps cancer outcomes, where weight loss and weakness predict worse survival.
